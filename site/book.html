The Book

I write most often in the darker hours.

I was writing long before anyone taught me how: poems sent to American radio stations from motel rooms in Detroit, stories posted on Writer’s Cafe under another name. I arrived at university with one already unfolding in me, and it became my dissertation, a short novel. It reads as fiction, and it’s a true story; both are so. The feedback was a sentence I had to ask about. Your writing is unyielding. I asked what unyielding meant. They told me. It was relentless from start to finish, and it left the reader exhausted. I left university feeling my degree was a failure.

After that I wrote books and destroyed them. Eighty thousand words open on the screen. Control, shift, delete. Then control, shift, save. Gone. I did that loads of times.

I’m not doing that with this one. This one lives. It has to live. I wanted to write a book that grows old with people, one they can’t bring themselves to throw away. If it helps you know yourself a little more, and feel more comfortable wherever you are, then I will be satisfied.

Wholeless Nights

Bipolar & the included middle

Mathew Pay

It’s 4:16am. His heart is racing, the dream is already fading, and the only thing to do is write.

Mathew Pay first heard the words “bipolar disorder” at eighteen, as a soldier on leave. He has been writing this book for seventeen years. Then, in a Johannesburg bookshop, a historian inscribed a book to him in isiZulu. When she translated it, it read: “The soldier who does not know himself, who does not have his own house.”

Wholeless Nights is written in 27 watches: nights spent awake, restorying old memories so they survive.

A journey across continents. An ancestral soldier who, 150 years ago, dropped the name Pay and enlisted as Bushell: the basket a light was hidden under. A dragon and a phoenix inked on one body. A circle of writers and a library of books that changed his world.

Wholeless is a boundless wisdom that keeps what is wholesome in mania, in depression, and in each in the other.

This is a book that builds a house and calls it home.

Writing for therapeutic purposes

It began at university, with Natalie Goldberg’s Writing Down the Bones, and a conference I volunteered at as an ambassador. Inspiring Transformations brought artists, writers and actors to the University of Northampton, to work with doctors, occupational therapists and psychologists. They spent the mornings on the evidence. In the afternoons the same people wrote, danced and took parts. In the evenings they sat at one table. Shaun McNiff was there. I’ve wanted to go further with it ever since.

It’s why I keep watch. I write what comes. Then I pause. Then I return to writing. Otherwise I can’t unfreeze myself, or stop myself. Writing things down, to me, is how I keep going. Walking is a little like writing for me. It’s a chance to really account for the moment. I just have to go even more slowly to write anything that makes sense.

Restorying

Restorying is telling a memory again, and differently, so it survives. What happened doesn’t change. Where I stand when I tell it does. Each watch takes one memory, brings it into the room at the hour, and tells it until it can be lived with, and what survives goes in. A creative writing degree and the arts and health movement gave me the word; the nights gave me the practice. It’s the belief under the book: we recover by restorying.

What it’s about, and the form

A man keeps watch through the night. He has no house, and does not know himself. A historian in a Johannesburg bookshop told him so, in isiZulu, in a book he’d asked her to sign. So he does what soldiers on watch have always done: he goes back through what he carries. Old books, old names, old selves are brought into the night one by one and tested. What survives goes in.

Nights at the watch, where earlier books and material are brought to be tested, and what survives goes in. The other stories become books within the book. It ends with a personal reflection on the process.

If a night is more than you can hold, reach a person first. Samaritans, 116 123, free, at any hour in the UK and Ireland. Elsewhere, findahelpline.com.

The watches

Twenty-seven nights. Every watch in first draft before Christmas 2026.

4 written2 still being written7 drafted, waiting for my voice3 arranged11 still to begin

1The Return

A GP surgery at eighteen, a mother explaining her son, a word the doctor offers. Then a minibus ride across Northampton, and a woman behind the glass who wants him to see. It's 04:16, and the book begins.

2The Names

One "t" or two. A clerk's note in the margin, an archive in Kent, and a soldier who gave up his surname in 1880. Then the dragon and the phoenix, inked on a day he won't dress up.

3The Inscription

Johannesburg, spring. A bookshop down a flight of steps, a launch, a question he decided not to ask. He asked for a dedication, and was given his own truth instead.

4The Pilgrimage

Step. Another. A motorbike he rides less fast now, a pilgrimage he read about on a plane and didn't walk, and a lake that teaches him how to be water.

5The Unyielding

"Your writing is unyielding," a tutor once said. Seventeen years of books written and deleted. Then he discovers what those seventeen years were really counting.

6Travelling Man

A care home, jacket potatoes, a quiet woman on the top floor. Before she died, she handed him her book. He's carried it ever since, as far as Africa.

7Chiba

A proposal written at night while the professors slept. An abstract about "Mr. Bushell," in the third person because it felt safe. Then the room in Japan where he had to say it as himself.

8The House Full of Other People's Children

Foster children came and went through one house on an estate, so many he lost count. A home where people were always temporary. A brother who left at sixteen, and why he understood.

9The Uniform

Seventeen and a half. Encryption keys you could drop and lose the message. Best soldier of two hundred. The mirror afterwards, and handing it all back.

10The Bridge

Detroit, 2008. A motel, poetry read to American radio, a stranger online, and a border he planned to cross and didn't.

11Two Emails

No GCSEs at twenty-six, and two emails that let him in anyway. A first year, a first conference, the first time in print.

12The Library

A library book with a tram ticket from 1932 inside. Fifty pence in a second-hand bookshop in Haworth. The books that came looking for him, and a chain of hands he's never joined up until now.

13The Kitchen Floor

Written four times before and never plainly. The centre of the book.

14The Magnet

A £1.50 horseshoe magnet, a small lie at a till, a park bench, and forty-seven printed pages that changed how he saw everything.

15Alan

"Nature herself never excludes you. Only some people, in some places do." Fourteen years of letters with the man who wrote it.

16Adam

A psychiatrist and pioneer of psychodrama, a stranger who answered his first letter the same day: two questors at the edge of the old ways of thinking. He pointed towards the caring professions, a road he was already on.

17The Asylum Garden

Night shifts where John Clare was kept, his grandfather's Penguin book, and a helicopter pilot who borrowed it and never gave it back.

18The Narrow Escape

The day everything stopped, and the day the drinking stopped with it. Two years of a programme, and a name for what bipolar never reached.

19Reclaiming Life

Frances Masters trained him as a therapeutic coach, his first step from lived experience into a profession. She named what he carried, and gave him her book. Three kinds of handing over from one woman.

20The Companion Writing Circle

A man on morning television, an email, a chapter that came of it. A weekly circle of thinkers, a chapter he was invited to write and didn't, and the first of them to die.

21NISA

A physicist who spoke of devotion and an old way of counting in the same breath. A way of counting and a way of loving in one conversation.

22Portobello

A long ride north, and a house by the sea where Louie Gardiner taught him presence in action, among people who let him see further than himself.

23Roy

Rev. Roy Reynolds wrote with many people, and with him. He offered four ways of thinking and doing, from fixing things to the inviting emptiness and beyond. Then his death.

24The Shape of Emotion

A pandemic, fifteen-hour days, and a comment on LinkedIn from a stranger who shared his name. A dining table, a photo of his daughters on the fridge, and happy tears for the first time in years.

25Vinnie

A peer in the support group, then the first person he coached, the case study behind his coaching submission, then a friend. Long silences between them, and then the online recovery community, where they shared networks and time again. This watch is for him.

26The Ones Who Came After

The work he does now, shaped by Vinnie: lived experience recovery organisations, where people who have been through it lead the way for those coming after.

27Afterword

Not a conclusion. Written from the desk after the last night: what he brought, what didn't survive, what he still doesn't know.

Matthew Bushell

Writer, peer and therapeutic coach

In Northampton, on UK time.

Northampton · England

My birth certificate says Mathew, with one t. My passport says Matthew, with two. No one in my family knows how the second t appeared. I found out in 2015, working at the county archives, when I held the original register in my hands and read what a government clerk had written beside my name.

Spelling of Mathew with one ‘T’ confirmed by both parents.In the margin. Original ink.

I kept looking. In the archives in Kent I found Stephen Bushell Pay, my third great-grandfather. When he enlisted in the British Army, some 150 years ago, he dropped Pay and went by Bushell. By 1880 he was in South Africa, in the First Boer War. Five generations of my forefathers served in the armed forces. At seventeen and a half, in the Royal Signals, I became the sixth.

I looked the names up as well. Matthew means gift. A bushel is the basket in Matthew’s gospel, the one you’re told not to hide your light under.

In September 2025 I went to South Africa too, to spend a week with my friends Chantal Dawtrey and Matthew Green of 5th Place. At Love Books in Johannesburg I queued to ask the historian Hlonipha Mokoena to sign her book, The Nightwatchman. I asked her to write it to the unknown, un-uniformed man. She looked at me, and wrote in isiZulu, and then she read it back to me in English.

The soldier who does not know himself, who does not have his own house.

Inscribed by Hlonipha Mokoena, Tuesday 9 September 2025

This is the house.

Come in.

I live with bipolar. The clinic calls it bipolar disorder. On my back is a dragon, and on the inside of my left arm a phoenix, inked over two days in Northampton in 2013. The dragon is mania. The phoenix is depression. For years I was told they were at war. Look closely and his wing is sheltering her.

I first heard the word at eighteen. It took ten years and four psychiatrists to become a diagnosis. Drink and drugs nearly finished me more than once; I know now I was trying to settle a nervous system in chaos. Since then there’s been a ward, an arrest, and six jobs offered and then withdrawn because of my risk.

The dragon and the phoenix live in me, each in the other. For a long time I felt I wasn’t whole, as if something in me was broken. I don’t see it that way now. I’m neither broken nor fixed, and I accept them both. That’s why I use the word wholeless. People hear it as wholeness, or as something missing, and it’s neither. It’s -⁠less as in boundless. Mania and depression are places I pass through. There’s no edge where I stop being me, and nowhere to send either of them.

The work comes out of all of it: bipolar, complex PTSD, substance use, the criminal justice system. Peer support gave me belonging. Therapeutic coaching gave me a way of working. Emotional fitness gave me something to do with a feeling, every day. From Johannesburg I flew on to Chiba, stood up at the International Society for Bipolar Disorders, and said it out loud: recovery is more than survival. It’s about finding a way to live that enables you to thrive.

Writing gets me through the night. The book is called Wholeless Nights, and I write it as Mathew Pay: the one t from the register, and the name Stephen Bushell Pay dropped when he enlisted. None of it happened alone.

The fourth place

Cyberspace

People can be met here too, when the tools let them.

The first computer in my life was a Commodore 64. I met the internet in Year 9, at grammar school, in 1995, and by fifteen I was talking with my friends on MSN. Later I made a game of it that I called Google adventures: put a phrase in quotation marks, search, and follow wherever it led, learning as I went, until I’d wandered into a field I knew nothing about.

At seventeen and a half the British Army handed me encryption keys: drop one and the message was gone. I’ve been inside networks ever since. After the army I built the networks that let factories see what their lines were doing, across the UK, Europe and the USA, and at night, in motels in Detroit, I wrote. A stranger online, and my belief that someone was waiting for me over the border in Canada, became the story I took to university.

Studying creative writing, I got curious about cyber-psychology. My dissertation, The Spirit of the Internet Guru, was that story: a man in a room, exchanging messages with an avatar, never sure who or what was on the other end. It reads as fiction, and it’s true. I handed it in in 2011, and the question has stayed with me: who is on the other end, and what do they need?

The work has a name now, digital social innovation. Nesta’s Our Tech, Our Future talks about tech as a tool in people’s hands, and that’s how I work: I take a technology into a room of people and make it work for them, around what matters in their lives. When I started out, there was no AI. Whatever came up was mine to deal with. No reference. No companion. Now I work on the other end of that old question. With David Pinto, on Pscale, in the open, I build with AI for other people, and what’s on the other end has a name, and the person keeps their own words.

The people on the other end

Twenty-six years of rooms, and who was in them.

2000

The Royal Signals

Analogue encryption keys: on, off, drop it and the message is gone. Secure links from headquarters out to the units. One of a few chosen to test Cormorant, the Army’s new mobile network, and to show it at St James’s Park.

2004

Factory floors

Data communications engineer. Production monitoring networks across the UK, Europe and the USA, so the people running a line could see what it was doing. I trained whoever had to use them and advised whoever ran the place.

2010

The Fishmarket Gallery

When the council didn’t renew the lease, I led the campaign to save the gallery, and the way we used social media to do it was new for its time.

2010

David Pinto

I started working with David, a mathematician with an idea about how people find each other. We’ve been at it, one way and another, ever since: Ecosquared, a social network where people passed their support forward to the work they valued; then Sqale, a pay-it-forward system, an alternative to the economics we’ve inherited; and now Pscale.

2011

St Andrew’s Healthcare

People living with brain injury and severe mental health conditions. Rehabilitation needed a measure of how much people actually moved. With the neurophysiotherapy team I brought physical activity monitors into the hospital’s clinical research, twenty thousand pounds’ worth of them, and the data opened up new strands of research, with clinicians including Dr Rafey Faruqui. I was lead researcher on the project, asking whether wearing a tri-axial monitor helps people change what they do for their health, and I ran the wellbeing programme that went with it.

2014

The county archives

I led the project that digitised Northamptonshire’s archaeological history and moved it to the cloud, so that researchers anywhere in the world could read it.

2016

Blinds, then software

A Northampton company taken through a rebrand and onto one software system, with its directors coached through the change. Then BlindMatrix, helping business owners take up new technology, with colleagues in Chennai. It was supposed to be my silver bullet. COVID changed me: fifteen hours a day, six days a week, deeply depressed, and finding comfort only in supporting the people around me.

2020

Bipolar Now

People living with bipolar, finding each other across time zones. A weekly podcast with Mike Lardi, and a peer support community of close to a thousand.

2022

Minderful

A health tech app for mental fitness, with Dr Nick Prior. As Head of Partnerships I took it to employers, charities, healthcare providers, a school and a community of start-up founders. Then I coached the people using it.

2022

Coaching clients

In my therapeutic coaching I use digital tools to develop therapeutic stories with the people I work with.

2024

Action for Happiness

People would come to a community of practice and talk, and things would get written down. Then they’d go, and weeks later an email would arrive saying what had happened. So I brought in tools that let people join in as it happened. Their insights were captured digitally and reflected back during the session, there to think about straight away.

2025

A charity and its stories

Staff and community members developing lived experience stories with each other. I gave them tools so they could be more present with each other. AI did the listening while they talked. Other AI, with guardrails, did the story development.

Pscale

Pscale is David Pinto’s invention. It’s a way of writing things down so that meaning has an address. A block is a small file, arranged from its broadest thought down to its finest detail, so a person or an AI can walk straight to what they need and leave the rest. People and AIs can read each other’s blocks, write to one another through them, and each keep their own account of their own life. It’s an open commons, in the lineage of Wikipedia and open source.

People walk and talk. AI and Pscale do the rest. David built the ground, and I’ve worked with him since 2010. He calls me the pilot.

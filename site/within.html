Within

In Northampton, on UK time.

A place is in me as much as I am in it. Water shapes the land and is shaped by it. Nothing in nature has a hard edge, and neither do I. So the place within is a space that receives, open on every side, where the day arrives and is met. My friends at 5th Place, who taught me emotional fitness, call the inner world the fifth place, the most important space of all. I hold it a little wider: all the places at once, each in the other, and it starts here. I spent years feeling shut out, of rooms, of the natural world, of myself, before I understood that this is the one place nobody can shut you out of. Nature herself never excludes you. Only some people, in some places, do.

I came to it the hard way. I live with bipolar, and for a long time the years were sorted into crises and recoveries, and nothing seemed to hold them together. Then, one night during my exams at university, I studied until I was badly distressed, and ended up on the kitchen floor. I woke there in the early hours after a vivid vision, and something had turned: for the first time I knew myself as a creative, living, evolving being. It was a breakdown and a breakthrough at once, each in the other, and later I learned the name for the state I’d entered: flow. That spring I went looking for anyone who could tell me what had happened to me, and wrote to a stranger, a psychiatrist called Adam Blatner, the night before an exam. He answered the same day and named a philosopher, Whitehead, whose book I became the first person to borrow from the university library. A year later a reply of Adam’s to a paper by Alan Rayner reached me by mistake. I wrote back that I was thankful either way, and fourteen years of letters began. What I’d felt on the kitchen floor, Alan had words for: natural inclusion. The search turned, from self recovery to self discovery.

Everything I’ve learned over those years comes down to one word: presence. This hour, this body, what’s in the room. Without it a day runs ahead of me, or I go under it. I lose it and I come back to it, and the coming back is the practice. Everything else on this page is a way back.

Presence in Action is Dr Louie Gardiner’s practice, and natural inclusion runs through it: Louie is one of the companions in Alan’s circle, and her doctorate on presence was written, in her words, in a naturally inclusional playspace. I rode a red Honda seven hours from Northampton to Portobello to train with her. Four days. I arrived carrying a heavy load and unsure I could set it down, and left able to untangle my facts, my feelings and my fictions. The people I met there were like windows. Through them I could see further than myself, and they could see into me without needing to fix anything.

She gave me six ways of looking at a day, with presence at the centre of them: what happened, what’s ahead, what you made of it, a choice, what matters, what you felt. I call them the six portals, and I keep my days through them. If I’m lost in what I’ve made of something, I ask myself what actually happened. If I’m racing ahead into what’s coming, I ask what matters. The question comes from the portal I’m not in, and it brings me back to the centre. That’s how presence is cultivated: by returning to it, again and again.

The feelings portal asked more of me than the others. Thinking I could do; a feeling I could only be carried by. Shape of Emotion gave me a way of handling one: find it in the body, give it a shape, and work with the shape, without first needing the story behind it. I learned it with Matthew Green and Chantal Dawtrey at 5th Place, and the first time I asked what shape a feeling was, I was sat at my own dining table. Once it had a shape it had somewhere to be, and so did I.

Looking through the portals is how I raise my awareness. Shape of Emotion is where I accept what I find, and that’s the harder part, because some of what I find I’d rather not have. Then action: a choice, and what’s ahead. For that I look across the five pillars of emotional fitness, thinking, feeling, moving, nourishing, relating, for where there’s room to strengthen, to grow, to deepen my wellbeing. Something to do with a feeling, every day. Awareness, acceptance, action. 5th Place gave me the order, and now I teach it. It’s what I mean when I say recovery is more than survival.

On my back is a dragon, and on the inside of my left arm a phoenix, inked over two days in Northampton in 2013. The dragon is mania. The phoenix is depression. For years I was told they were at war. Look closely and his wing is sheltering her.

For a long time I felt I wasn’t whole, as if something in me was broken. I don’t see it that way now. I’m neither broken nor fixed, and I accept them both. That’s why I made a word: wholeless, -⁠less as in boundless, as in endless. One axis, two ends, and nothing outside it. I live in both mania and depression, each in the other, and nothing has to be cut away for me to be me. It’s natural inclusion lived from the inside: never finished, never bounded, always in motion.

I wrote one day that I may have finally found a way to turn the horrors of the night into hope for the day. Within is the one thing I carry into home, work, community and cyberspace, and the place I come back to, every day, in every moment. And when a day won’t settle, I write. I was writing long before I studied it: poems sent to American radio stations from a motel in Detroit, a story already under way when I arrived at university. Writing is the surest way I know back into flow: the hour, the body, what’s in the room, and then the next sentence. The book →
